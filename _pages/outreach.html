Outreach: the Electron Ejector

The Electron Ejector looks like a carnival hi-striker, but don't smash it... all that counts is the colour of your hammer.

This is a portable demonstration of the photoelectric effect and wave-particle duality. Players pick a metal, then choose a hammer with a specific frequency (red, green, or violet light) and see whether it has enough energy to knock an electron free.

Try it

This is a small browser version of the real game, with the same metals, hammers, and screens. Press Select to change metal, plug in a hammer, and press Hit. Can you work out which hammers work on which metals?

The Photoelectric Effect

When light shines on a metal, electrons can escape from it. This is the photoelectric effect, and it was one of the first pieces of evidence for quantum theory. What you'd expect to happen depends on how you think light behaves.

If light is only a wave

Energy flows into the electrons continuously, so any colour of light should eject them eventually. Brighter light would just do it faster.

If light comes in packets

Each photon interacts with one electron. Either it has enough energy to free the electron or it doesn't, and energy can't build up from photon to photon.

Experiments support the second picture. Below a threshold frequency, which is different for each metal, no electrons are ejected. But light also behaves like a wave, as when players look through a prism before picking up a hammer, dispersion spreads white light into a spectrum. Quantum theory brings both of these descriptions together.

Light-matter interactions are important in modern research, as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy uses it to probe the composition and electronic structure of materials, and our own research relies on this understanding.

Build your own

The Electron Ejector is made from low-cost materials and open-source electronics. It folds at a hinge and fits in a suitcase, so it can travel by car or train, and it takes about 30 minutes to put together on site.

  • Frame: plywood, timber, and 3D-printed parts
  • Computer: a Raspberry Pi 4B running Python
  • Display: a 15.6 inch screen and LED strips in a light-diffusing channel
  • Controls: Select and Hit arcade buttons, and hammers that plug into barrel jacks

Make your hammers sturdy, as some players really do treat it like a carnival game.

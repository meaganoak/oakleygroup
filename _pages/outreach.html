Outreach: the Electron Ejector

A carnival hi-striker with a quantum twist. Hit it as hard as you like: the only thing that matters is the colour of your hammer.

The Electron Ejector is a portable, hands-on demonstration of the photoelectric effect and wave-particle duality. Players pick a metal, choose a hammer that stands in for red, green, or violet light, and find out whether it has enough energy to knock an electron free.

We built it to give the public a fun way into quantum science, and to give A-level students an interactive way to learn ideas that are usually taught on a whiteboard. The code, 3D models, and build instructions are all open, so you can make your own.

Try it

This is a small browser version of the real thing, using the same metals, hammers, and screens. Press Select to change metal, plug in a hammer, and press Hit. Can you work out which hammers work on which metals?

What's going on?

Shine light on a metal and electrons can escape from it. This is the photoelectric effect, and it was one of the first pieces of evidence for quantum theory. The way it behaves is the surprise, because it depends on what you think light is.

If light is only a wave

Energy flows into the electrons continuously, so any colour of light should eject them in the end. Brighter light would just do it faster.

If light comes in packets

Each photon meets an electron one-on-one. It either has enough energy to free the electron, or it doesn't, and energy can't build up from photon to photon.

Experiments side with the packets. Below a threshold frequency, which is different for each metal, no electrons come out at all. Light does still behave like a wave, though: look through a prism, as players do before they pick up a hammer, and dispersion spreads white light into a spectrum. We need both pictures, and quantum theory is what lets them sit together.

This isn't just history. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy uses the same effect to probe the composition and electronic structure of materials, and light-matter interactions are at the heart of our own research on X-ray spectra.

Build your own

The Electron Ejector is made from low-cost materials and open-source electronics, and it folds at a hinge to pack into a suitcase for the car or the train. It takes about 30 minutes to put together on site.

  • Frame: plywood, timber, and 3D-printed parts
  • Brains: a Raspberry Pi 4B running Python
  • Output: a 15.6 inch display and LED strips in a light-diffusing channel
  • Input: Select and Hit arcade buttons, and hammers that plug into barrel jacks

A word of advice from experience: make the hammers sturdy. Some players take the carnival game very literally.

Outreach: the Electron Ejector

The Electron Ejector looks like a carnival hi-striker, but don't smash it... instead all that counts is the colour of your hammer.

A portable demonstration of the photoelectric effect and wave-particle duality. Players pick a metal, then choose a certain frequency hammer (red, green, or violet light), and see whether it has enough energy to knock an electron free.

We built it to introduce the public to quantum science, and so that A-level students could try out ideas they usually only see on paper. The code, 3D models, and build instructions are all open source, so you can make your own.

The Photoelectric Effect

When light of a specific frequency shines on a metal, electrons are ejected. This is the photoelectric effect, and it was one of the first pieces of evidence for quantum theory. What you'd expect to happen depends on how you think light behaves.

If light is only a wave

Energy flows into the electrons continuously, so any colour of light should eject them eventually and more intense light would just do it faster.

If light comes in packets

Each photon interacts with one electron. Either it has enough energy to free the electron or it doesn't, and energy can't build up from photon to photon.

Below a threshold frequency, which is different for each metal, no electrons come out at all. But light also behaves like a wave: when players look through a prism before picking up a hammer, dispersion spreads white light into a spectrum. We need both descriptions, and quantum theory brings them together.

Modern charactertisation techniques still use this effect today. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy uses it to probe the composition and electronic structure of materials, and our own research on X-ray spectra also relies on understanding how light and matter interact.

Build your own

The Electron Ejector is made from low-cost materials and open-source electronics. It folds at a hinge and fits in a suitcase, so it can travel by car or train, and it takes about 30 minutes to put together on site.

  • Frame: plywood, timber, and 3D-printed parts
  • Computer: a Raspberry Pi 4B running Python
  • Display: a 15.6 inch screen and LED strips in a light-diffusing channel
  • Controls: Select and Hit arcade buttons, and hammers that plug into barrel jacks

Make your hammers sturdy, as some players really do treat it like a carnival game.
